## 3. Rationale

**Working thesis:** The rights that shape a modern life — how your data is used, whether an algorithm can decide whether you get a loan or a job, whether the government can read your messages, whether you can see the code that runs a public service — are not addressed by the 1791 Bill of Rights. That's not a criticism of the founders. It's a gap that a serious constitutional refresh has to close.

## 8. Open questions

Expected topics this amendment must address. Each will need its own section, its own trade-off analysis, and possibly its own competing draft:

- **Data ownership and portability.** Who owns data about you? Can you take it with you?
- **Algorithmic transparency.** When an algorithm makes a decision about you, what do you have a right to know? What do you have a right to contest?
- **Right to encryption.** Can the government require backdoors? What about the right to run code that resists inspection?
- **Freedom from mass surveillance.** Warrant requirements for digital collection at scale. Bans on suspicionless bulk collection.
- **Public digital infrastructure.** Do citizens have a right to non-corporate digital identity, public broadband, open-source public services?
- **Right to be forgotten (US version).** Under what circumstances can you require deletion? How does this interact with the First Amendment?
- **Protection from algorithmic discrimination.** Disparate-impact protections for automated decision-making.
- **AI disclosure.** When you're interacting with a machine, do you have a right to know?
