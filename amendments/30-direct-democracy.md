# Amendment 30 — Direct Digital Democracy

**Model C — Hybrid.** Abolish the House of Representatives. Keep a small, reformed Senate as a technical and continuity body. Direct democracy replaces the House's general legislative function.

> This file is one of three competing drafts of Amendment 30. The other two — Pure Direct Democracy (Model A) and Sortition (Model B) — live on separate branches. The repo will pick one, or merge the best of all three.

---

## Part 1 — The text

### Section 1. Establishment

The legislative power of the United States is vested jointly in the People of the United States, acting directly through a verified national vote, and in the Senate of the United States, reconstituted as set forth in this Article.

The House of Representatives is abolished. All legislative authority previously held by the House passes to the People.

### Section 2. Eligibility to vote

Every citizen of the United States who has attained the age of eighteen years is entitled to one vote on every federal legislative matter put to direct vote.

The right to vote shall not be denied or abridged on account of race, color, sex, religion, national origin, disability, sexual orientation, gender identity, wealth, place of residence within the United States, criminal history, or political belief.

### Section 3. The reformed Senate

#### 3.1 Composition

The Senate shall consist of one hundred (100) members, two from each State, elected by the people of that State.

#### 3.2 Term

Senators serve a single term of six years. No Senator may serve more than one term in any lifetime.

#### 3.3 Election

Senatorial elections shall be conducted by ranked-choice or equivalent preferential voting. No campaign shall be funded by private money. All Senate campaigns shall be funded by an equal allocation from the Federal Election Authority, with strict expenditure caps and complete public disclosure.

No Senator may accept any compensation, gift, or thing of value above a de minimis threshold from any private source during their term and for ten years thereafter. Violation is a felony.

No Senator may engage in employment with any federal contractor, registered foreign agent, or entity having business before the Senate during their term and for ten years thereafter.

#### 3.4 Powers and duties

The Senate shall:

1. Propose, debate, and pass federal statutes within its concurrent legislative authority under Section 5.
2. Approve the federal budget, with line-item authority.
3. Confirm executive and judicial nominees.
4. Ratify treaties by two-thirds vote.
5. Conduct oversight of the Executive branch, with subpoena power.
6. Declare war, subject to direct ratification under Section 5.

#### 3.5 Conduct

All Senate proceedings shall be public. All votes shall be recorded by name. All communications between Senators and registered lobbyists are prohibited; all other outside communications on matters of legislation shall be logged and published within seventy-two hours.

Registered lobbying as a profession is abolished. Any person may petition the Senate or any Senator in writing, and all such petitions shall be public.

### Section 4. Proposals from the People

Any citizen may propose federal legislation by direct ballot. A proposal becomes a national ballot measure when it is supported by a number of citizens equal to one half of one percent (0.5%) of the previous national vote total.

Every citizen has the right to use AI drafting assistance, provided at public expense, to prepare proposed legislation.

### Section 5. Concurrent authority and direct ratification

#### 5.1 What the Senate may pass alone

The Senate may pass laws governing:

1. The federal budget and appropriations.
2. The structure and administration of executive departments.
3. The confirmation of nominees.
4. Treaty ratification.
5. Continuous regulatory authorization.

#### 5.2 What requires direct ratification by the People

The following acts require ratification by direct vote of the People under Section 6:

1. Declarations of war and extensions of armed conflict beyond ninety days.
2. Federal tax changes raising or lowering revenue by more than one percent of gross domestic product.
3. Changes to criminal law.
4. Constitutional amendments proposed by the Senate.
5. Any measure passed by the Senate that ten percent of the previous national vote total signs a petition to put to direct vote within thirty days of passage.

#### 5.3 What the People may pass alone

The People may, by ballot measure under Section 6, pass any federal statute, override any Senate act, or place a question before the Senate for action.

### Section 6. Direct voting procedure

#### 6.1 Deliberation

A ballot measure shall be deliberated for not less than ninety days before a vote, during which time:

1. An impact analysis shall be prepared by not fewer than three independent public AI systems, with disclosed training data and source code, and published in full.
2. A plain-language summary, not exceeding one page, shall accompany the full text.
3. A disclosure of every person, organization, and expenditure supporting or opposing the measure shall be published and kept current.
4. Public deliberation forums shall be open to all citizens, in both digital and physical form, with paper participation available in every county.

#### 6.2 Voting

Voting on a ballot measure shall remain open for not less than fourteen consecutive days. A measure passes if:

1. A majority of votes cast favor it, and
2. The number of votes cast equals or exceeds twenty-five percent of eligible voters.

For Major measures as defined in Section 5.2, the threshold is sixty percent of votes cast and a quorum of forty percent of eligible voters.

Voting shall be conducted by means that are secure, auditable, accessible to citizens without internet access, and verifiable on paper.

### Section 7. Limits

Neither the People by direct vote nor the Senate by its own act may:

1. Abridge any right protected by this Constitution.
2. Convict any person of a crime, or impose punishment on any named person.
3. Suspend the writ of habeas corpus, except as elsewhere provided in this Constitution.
4. Pass any bill of attainder or ex post facto law.
5. Override a final judgment of the Supreme Court on a constitutional question.

### Section 8. Emergency powers

In a declared national emergency, the President may, with the concurrence of the Vice President and the Chief Justice of the United States, issue temporary measures with the force of law for a period not exceeding thirty days. Such measures shall expire automatically and may be extended only by action of the Senate, ratified by the People under Section 6.

### Section 9. Judicial review

All acts under this Article are subject to judicial review for conformity with this Constitution. Courts shall not strike down such acts on grounds of policy disagreement, but only for constitutional violation.

### Section 10. Sunset

Every act under this Article expires ten years from the date of passage, unless renewed by the same process by which it was originally passed.

### Section 11. Enabling legislation

The Federal Election Authority shall administer Senate elections, manage direct voting, and operate the public AI drafting and analysis systems. It shall be funded independently of the executive branch and shall be auditable by any citizen.

## Part 2 — Plain-language commentary

**What this does in everyday terms:**

- **The House of Representatives is gone.** That's where most of the capture lives — 435 seats, two-year terms, constant fundraising, gerrymandered districts. We don't fix it. We replace it.
- **You replace the House.** Direct democracy. Your vote is the new House.
- **The Senate stays, but transformed.** 100 Senators. One term of 6 years. Ever. Then you go home.
- **No private money in Senate elections.** Public funding only. Equal allocation. Strict caps.
- **Ranked-choice voting.** No more "vote for the lesser evil." Rank your real preferences.
- **Lobbying as a profession is illegal.** You can write to a Senator. So can anyone. All letters are public.
- **No Senator can take corporate money or jobs for 10 years after serving.** Felony if they do.
- **The Senate handles the boring, continuous stuff.** Budgets, oversight, confirmations. Direct democracy is bad at these.
- **You override the Senate on big stuff.** Direct vote required for: war, big tax changes, criminal law. And anything 10% of voters petition to ballot.
- **Major direct votes need 60% + 40% turnout.** Standard ones need simple majority + 25% turnout.

**What this does not do:**

- It does not preserve the current Senate. The current Senate is part of the problem. This is a reconstituted Senate with single terms, public funding only, no lobbying, and a 10-year cooling-off period. It is not the Senate you know.
- It does not require you to vote on every issue. The Senate does continuous work. You vote on what matters most.
- It does not eliminate the Executive or the Courts.

## Part 3 — Rationale

### Why hybrid

This is the most conservative of the three models, by design. It keeps something resembling a representative body — a small, reformed Senate — and pairs it with direct democracy. We argue for it on three grounds.

**1. Continuity matters.** Federal governance involves treaties that span decades, regulatory regimes that need stable oversight, and budget cycles that build on each other year over year. A small standing body with elected members and professional staff is the most reliable mechanism we know for sustained, technical work.

**2. Familiarity matters politically.** Of the three models, this one is the easiest to explain at a town hall. "We're keeping the Senate, fixing it, abolishing the House, and replacing it with you" is one sentence. The Pure DD and Sortition pitches each require ten minutes of explanation before people stop bristling. Politics rewards models that fit on a bumper sticker.

**3. Risk diversification.** Direct democracy can fail in specific ways (mob, fatigue, brigading). A reformed elected body fails in different ways (capture, complacency, careerism). Combining them means a failure mode in one is partially backstopped by the other. The risk profile is less concentrated.

### Why abolish the House

The House is where capture is densest. Two-year cycles mean fundraising never stops. 435 seats mean lobbyists can pick targets cheaply. Gerrymandering makes most districts safe, so the only meaningful election is the primary, and primaries reward extreme positions and donor loyalty. The result is a body that is structurally incapable of passing what majorities of the country want.

Direct democracy does what the House was originally designed to do — let the population speak on legislation — without the intermediary layer that has become the failure point.

### Why keep the Senate, transformed

The Senate as it exists today has the same capture problems. But the Senate as designed — a smaller body with longer terms, doing slower, more deliberative work — is closer to a defensible model. The transformations in Section 3 are the difference:

- **Single term, no re-election.** Removes the career-politician incentive that drives most capture. A Senator who can never run again has no donors to please.
- **100% public funding, ranked-choice elections.** Removes the donor primary. Removes spoilers. Picks the candidate the median voter actually prefers, not the candidate party loyalists prefer.
- **Banned outright corporate compensation, 10-year cooling off.** Removes the "future job at a lobbying firm" incentive structure that drives policy in the current Senate.
- **No registered lobbyists.** Anyone can petition, in writing, publicly. Lobbying as a paid profession is over.
- **Logged outside communications, public proceedings, recorded votes.** Transparency replaces backroom dealmaking.

This is not the Senate you know. It is closer to what the Senate was supposed to be.

### What this model is good at

- **Continuity.** Senators serve six years. The Senate as a body has institutional memory across decades.
- **Familiarity.** Easier to sell, easier to transition to, harder to caricature.
- **Capacity for sustained technical work.** Budgets, oversight, confirmations, regulatory authorization — work that direct democracy is genuinely bad at.
- **Backstops direct democracy.** When direct democracy fails (low turnout, brigading, fatigue), the Senate is still doing baseline legislative work.

### What this model is bad at — and we are not hiding this

**It still includes elections.** Elections, even publicly funded and ranked-choice, still favor people who can perform charisma on camera, who have time and resources to campaign, and who have name recognition. Sortition advocates argue that this filter is itself the problem. We are not sure they're wrong; we are betting that radical campaign-finance reform changes the filter enough.

**Single-term Senators have no accountability through re-election.** If a Senator goes rogue, voters can't punish them at the ballot box. The counter is: voters never really did. A Senator with deep donor backing in a safe state was effectively unaccountable. Public funding, recall procedures (to be defined by enabling legislation), and the 10-year cooling-off period are the mechanisms here, not re-election.

**"Reformed Senate" sounds like a half-measure.** It is a half-measure, by design. We are betting that the best politically viable path is one radical change (abolish the House, direct democracy) and one reform (transformed Senate), rather than two radical changes simultaneously.

**Capture migration.** Eliminating the House without eliminating the Senate concentrates lobbying pressure on a smaller body. Section 3.5's bright-line ban on registered lobbying is meant to address this. Whether the ban survives litigation under existing First Amendment doctrine is a real question. Our position: petitioning the government is a right; *being paid* to petition the government is a profession that can be regulated.

**Senators capturing direct democracy.** A Senate that wants to expand its own power could use its agenda-setting authority to crowd out direct measures. The 10% petition override in Section 5.2 is the citizen-side firewall. It can be argued whether 10% is the right number.

**Mob rule risk on direct votes.** Same as Models A and B. Section 7 firewall and judicial review under Section 9.

### The serious case against this model

The strongest argument against the hybrid is that it doesn't go far enough. Sortition advocates will say: you've kept the very mechanism — elections to a small standing body — that produced the capture you're trying to escape. The transformations are real, but elections are gravitational. Donors, parties, and incumbency advantages will reassert themselves over decades, even with public funding and term limits.

We take that seriously. The honest answer is: maybe. The hybrid is a bet that radical campaign finance reform plus single terms plus a 10-year cooling-off period plus public funding plus ranked-choice voting plus lobbying abolition is enough new structure to break the capture cycle. If we're wrong, the system degrades back toward the current one over decades — and direct democracy is still there as a backstop.

The other models are betting that elections themselves are the problem. They may be right. This model is the bet that elections can be salvaged with enough reform.

### The competing models

This is Model C. Two siblings exist:

- **Model A — Pure Direct Democracy.** Citizens vote on everything directly. No standing body at all.
- **Model B — Sortition.** Replace Congress with a randomly selected People's Assembly of 400. Direct democracy retains override authority.

Read all three. Argue. Vote.
