# Amendment 30 — Direct Digital Democracy

**Model A — Pure Direct Democracy.** Congress is abolished. Citizens vote directly on federal legislation, assisted by AI drafting tools, with constitutional firewalls against mob rule.

> This file is one of three competing drafts of Amendment 30. The other two — Sortition (Model B) and Hybrid (Model C) — live on separate branches. The repo will pick one, or merge the best of all three.

---

## Part 1 — The text

### Section 1. Establishment

The legislative power of the United States is vested in the People of the United States, acting directly through a verified national vote.

The Congress of the United States, including the Senate and the House of Representatives, is abolished. All legislative authority previously held by Congress passes to the People.

### Section 2. Eligibility

Every citizen of the United States who has attained the age of eighteen years is entitled to one vote on every federal legislative matter. No citizen's vote shall count for more than one, and no citizen's vote shall count for less.

The right to vote shall not be denied or abridged on account of race, color, sex, religion, national origin, disability, sexual orientation, gender identity, wealth, place of residence within the United States, criminal history, or political belief.

### Section 3. Proposals

Any citizen may propose federal legislation. A proposal becomes a national ballot measure when it is supported by a number of citizens equal to one half of one percent (0.5%) of the previous national vote total.

Every citizen has the right to use AI drafting assistance, provided at public expense, to prepare proposed legislation. No citizen's proposal shall be rejected for form or style if its meaning is clear.

### Section 4. Deliberation

A ballot measure shall be deliberated for not less than ninety days before a vote, during which time:

1. An impact analysis shall be prepared by not fewer than three independent public AI systems, with disclosed training data and source code, and published in full.
2. A plain-language summary, not exceeding one page, shall accompany the full text.
3. A disclosure of every person, organization, and expenditure supporting or opposing the measure shall be published and kept current.
4. Public deliberation forums shall be open to all citizens, in both digital and physical form, with paper participation available in every county.

### Section 5. Voting

Voting on a ballot measure shall remain open for not less than fourteen consecutive days.

A measure passes if:

1. A majority of votes cast favor it, and
2. The number of votes cast equals or exceeds twenty-five percent of eligible voters.

For measures designated as Major (defined by enabling legislation, and including at minimum any measure committing the United States to armed conflict, raising or lowering federal taxes by more than one percent of gross domestic product, or amending criminal law), the passing threshold is sixty percent of votes cast and a quorum of forty percent of eligible voters.

Voting shall be conducted by means that are secure, auditable, accessible to citizens without internet access, and verifiable on paper.

### Section 6. Limits on direct legislation

The People, acting under this Article, may not by direct vote:

1. Abridge any right protected by this Constitution.
2. Convict any person of a crime, or impose punishment on any named person.
3. Suspend the writ of habeas corpus, except as elsewhere provided in this Constitution.
4. Pass any bill of attainder or ex post facto law.
5. Declare war for a duration exceeding ninety days without re-vote.
6. Override a final judgment of the Supreme Court on a constitutional question.

### Section 7. Emergency powers

In a declared national emergency, the President may, with the concurrence of the Vice President and the Chief Justice of the United States, issue temporary measures with the force of law for a period not exceeding thirty days. Such measures shall expire automatically and may be extended only by a vote of the People under Section 5.

No emergency declaration shall suspend the right to vote, abridge the freedoms protected by the First Amendment, or extend the term of any office.

### Section 8. Judicial review

Measures passed under this Article are subject to judicial review for conformity with this Constitution. Courts shall not strike down such measures on grounds of policy disagreement, but only for constitutional violation.

### Section 9. Sunset

Every measure passed under this Article expires ten years from the date of passage, unless renewed by a vote of the People under Section 5.

### Section 10. Enabling legislation

The First Congress under this Article — meaning the first national vote — shall establish the offices, procedures, and protections necessary to administer this Article, including a non-partisan Federal Election Authority, identity verification standards, accessibility requirements, and the operation of public AI drafting and analysis systems.

The Federal Election Authority shall be funded independently of the executive branch and shall be auditable by any citizen.

## Part 2 — Plain-language commentary

**What this does in everyday terms:**

- **You vote on the laws.** Not someone you elected. You. On your phone, in person, on paper — your choice.
- **Congress is gone.** No Senate. No House. No representatives whose job is to vote on your behalf.
- **You can write a law.** If you can get a half a percent of last election's voters to agree it should go on the ballot, it goes on the ballot.
- **AI helps you draft.** For free. Paid for publicly. So a single citizen has the same drafting horsepower as any K Street firm.
- **You have at least 90 days to read it.** Plus a one-page summary. Plus an impact analysis from three independent AIs whose code is public. Plus a public list of every dollar spent for or against.
- **Voting is open for two weeks.** Not one day. So you don't have to take time off work.
- **Most things pass on a majority** with at least 1 in 4 eligible voters showing up. Big things — war, big tax changes, criminal law — need 60% and 2 in 5 showing up.
- **Some things you can't vote away.** You can't vote to strip a constitutional right. You can't vote to throw a specific person in jail. You can't suspend the writ of habeas corpus. The Bill of Rights still wins.
- **Emergencies have a 30-day hard cap** before you have to vote on extending them.
- **Every law expires in 10 years** unless you re-vote to keep it.
- **Courts can still strike laws down — but only on constitutional grounds, not because they disagree with the policy.**

**What this does not do:**

- It does not get rid of the President, the Cabinet, or the Courts. They still administer and adjudicate. You write the law.
- It does not require you to vote on every measure. Skip it. Skip them all if you want. Other citizens will show up; the quorum requirements make sure enough of them do, or nothing passes.
- It does not turn the country into a video game. The deliberation period, the impact analysis, the disclosure of money — those are the slow, boring, honest parts that make this work.

## Part 3 — Rationale

### Why we wrote this

The current Congress does not work for ordinary Americans. That is not opinion. It is decades of polling, voting behavior, donor analysis, and outcome measurement. A small majority of Americans support things — Medicare for all, paid family leave, capping prescription drug prices, breaking up monopolies, taxing extreme wealth — that Congress consistently does not deliver. The reason is not a mystery. The pipeline from voter to law is mediated by 535 people who are themselves mediated by donors, lobbyists, and party leadership.

Representative democracy was a workable compromise for 1789. In 1789, the country had four million people, communication moved at the speed of a horse, and there was no way for everyone to vote on everything. Representatives were a delivery mechanism, not a sacred principle.

We now have the technology to vote directly, deliberate at scale, and equip every citizen with drafting tools as good as any lobbyist's. The compromise of representative democracy is no longer necessary. And the cost of it — capture — has become unacceptable.

### Why we're abolishing Congress entirely

The smaller move is to keep Congress and add direct democracy as an override. We rejected that for three reasons:

**1. Two systems compete; one wins.** If Congress can pass laws and citizens can pass laws, the system with the better-funded actors wins the fights it picks. Congress, with lobbyists, will out-resource citizen ballot measures every time except the most viral ones. Keeping Congress is keeping capture.

**2. A half-reform is a target.** Hybrid systems get rolled back. A clean break is harder to undo. The political cost of restoring Congress is much higher than the political cost of weakening direct democracy from within. We want the harder-to-undo version.

**3. The expertise argument has not held up.** Congress is not an expert body. It is a body of generalists supported by staff, lobbyists, and outside research. Citizens with AI drafting tools, three independent impact analyses, and a 90-day deliberation period have access to *better* analysis than the median member of Congress reads before voting.

### What this model is good at

- **It is honest.** It says: the people rule. No intermediary. No proxy. No misdirection about who actually decides.
- **It is hard to capture.** You cannot lobby 200 million people the way you can lobby 535. The capture model that has worked since the 1970s does not scale to a national electorate.
- **It is participatory.** A citizen who wants to propose a law can. With public drafting help. Without raising money. Without joining a party.
- **It is auditable.** Every vote, every dollar, every AI analysis is public.

### What this model is bad at — and we are not hiding this

**Voter fatigue.** If 50 ballot measures land in a year, attention collapses. The quorum and supermajority requirements partially mitigate this — if nobody's paying attention, nothing passes — but they don't solve it.

**Mob rule risk.** This is the serious objection, and we will not pretend it isn't. Direct democracies have voted to do terrible things. California's Proposition 8. Brexit. Athenian votes to execute generals. A majority can be wrong. A majority can be cruel.

Our answer is the Bill of Rights firewall in Section 6 and judicial review in Section 8. The People cannot vote to strip rights. They cannot vote to imprison a named person. They cannot vote away habeas corpus. They cannot override the Supreme Court on constitutional questions.

That is not a complete answer. A determined majority can amend the Constitution and dismantle those firewalls. The honest position is: no system survives a sustained majority that wants to destroy it. Representative democracy doesn't either; the path is just slower and less visible. We choose the system that fails visibly over the system that fails quietly.

**Sustained, technical work.** Budget detail. Treaty negotiation. Confirmations. Continuous oversight. Direct democracy is bad at these by nature — they require continuous attention from specialists. This amendment leaves them with the Executive (administered by Cabinet-level departments) and the Courts. That is a real concession; it means more power concentrates in the Executive than today. Other amendments in this slate (judicial reform, anti-capture, civic sunset) are designed in part to balance that.

**Digital divide.** Voting must work without smartphones, without high-speed internet, without literacy in English. Section 5 requires paper-verifiable, accessible voting. Section 4 requires paper participation in every county. These are not afterthoughts. They are the difference between direct democracy and tech-bro democracy.

**AI bias.** Three independent AI analyses with disclosed training data and open source code is our best current answer. It is not a perfect answer. AI alignment is an unsolved field. We are betting that *open* AI assistance with multiple independent providers is better than the alternative of *no* assistance, where lobbyists and corporate counsel dominate by default.

### The competing models

This is Model A. Two siblings exist:

- **Model B — Sortition.** Replace Congress with a randomly selected citizen body serving fixed terms. Direct democracy handles statute; the sortition body handles budget, oversight, confirmations.
- **Model C — Hybrid.** Keep a small elected technical Senate for sustained work. Abolish the House. Direct democracy replaces the House's general legislative function.

Read all three. Argue. Vote.

### What it would take to make this real

This amendment requires:

- A national identity-verification system robust enough that one person equals one vote, without becoming a surveillance apparatus.
- A Federal Election Authority that runs voting with paper backup in every jurisdiction.
- Publicly funded AI drafting and analysis systems whose code is open and whose biases are auditable.
- Massive civic education before the first vote.

None of these are easy. None of them are impossible. We are not the first generation to face hard infrastructure problems.
