## 3. Rationale

**Working thesis:** Software teams learned the hard way that code you never revisit rots. Statutes rot the same way — they accrete carve-outs, they lose the context that justified them, and they lock in the assumptions of a world that no longer exists. A sunset default flips the burden: instead of a law surviving forever unless someone can muster the political capital to repeal it, a law expires unless someone can muster the political capital to reauthorize it. Same energy, opposite direction.

## 7. Objections and responses

**TODO.** Anticipated objections:
- **Legislative overload.** Congress can barely pass a budget; how will it reauthorize every law every ten years?
- **Instability of long-term planning.** Contracts, entitlements, and multi-decade programs need durability.
- **Weaponization.** A minority faction can kill a law simply by blocking reauthorization.
- **Rights sunsets are unacceptable.** Some things — anti-discrimination law, voting rights protections — cannot be allowed to lapse for procedural reasons.

## 8. Open questions

Expected components:

- **The sunset window.** 10 years default? Different for different categories?
- **What's exempt.** Rights-protecting statutes, treaty obligations, entitlement structures — some things must not sunset. Which?
- **The reauthorization process.** Automatic renewal absent objection? Affirmative vote required? Public-comment stage?
- **Presumption on failure.** If Congress simply fails to act, does the law lapse, extend by one term, or extend indefinitely?
- **Regulatory sunset.** Does this extend to executive regulations, or only statutes?
- **Interaction with Amendment 30.** In a direct-democracy world, sunset reauthorizations could become the primary vehicle of citizen legislation.
