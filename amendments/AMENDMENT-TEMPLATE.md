## 1. The text

*This is the section that would go into the Constitution. Every word counts. Keep it short. Keep it plain. Every clause should survive being read out loud to a stranger.*

> **Section 1.** [Core right or power being established.]
>
> **Section 2.** [Scope: who this applies to, where, when.]
>
> **Section 3.** [Limits, exceptions, and how conflicts are resolved.]
>
> **Section 4.** [Enforcement: who enforces it, how, and what the remedy is when it's violated.]
>
> **Section 5.** [Congress shall have power to enforce this article by appropriate legislation.] *(Standard enforcement clause where applicable.)*

**Word count target:** under 400 words for the constitutional text itself. If you can't say it in 400 words, you probably haven't figured out what you're saying yet.

---

## 2. Plain-language commentary

*What does this actually mean for a person living their life? Write this like you're explaining it to a neighbor over a fence.*

- **What it does:** In one paragraph, in everyday words.
- **What it doesn't do:** Common misreadings, cleared up front.
- **A concrete example:** One realistic scenario where this amendment changes an outcome.
- **A second concrete example:** One where it *doesn't* change an outcome, so scope is clear.

---

## 3. Rationale

*What problem does this solve? Why now? Why an amendment and not a statute?*

### 3.1 The problem

State the specific harm this amendment addresses. Cite evidence. Numbers, cases, documented patterns — not vibes.

### 3.2 Why current law fails

What has been tried. What courts have ruled. Why statutes and regulations haven't been enough (or have been rolled back).

### 3.3 Why a constitutional amendment

Amendments are the heaviest instrument in the toolbox. Explain why nothing lighter works here. If a statute would do, this shouldn't be an amendment.

---

## 4. Prior art

*Has anyone tried something like this? What happened?*

- **In the United States:** State constitutions, prior amendment attempts, related SCOTUS cases.
- **In other countries:** Comparable rights or structures elsewhere, and how they've performed.
- **In private governance:** Corporate charters, cooperative structures, open-source project constitutions — if relevant.

Cite everything with links.

---

## 5. Expected effects

*Honest accounting. Who wins. Who loses. What breaks.*

### 5.1 Who benefits and how

Be specific. Not "the American people." Which people, in which situations.

### 5.2 Who bears costs

Every real amendment has losers. Name them. Estimate the size of the burden. Don't pretend it's free.

### 5.3 Second-order effects

What behaviors, incentives, or institutions will shift as a result. Where might this be gamed. Where might it backfire.

### 5.4 Implementation cost

Rough estimate of what it takes to enforce this in dollars, staffing, and time. If we don't know, say we don't know.

---

## 6. Interaction with existing law

*What in the current Constitution, US Code, or major SCOTUS precedent does this touch, override, or reinforce?*

- **Amends or overrides:** Specific clauses, statutes, or holdings displaced.
- **Reinforces:** Rights or structures this shores up.
- **Ambiguous overlap:** Areas where courts will have to sort it out. Acknowledge them.

## 8. Open questions

*What haven't we figured out yet? What are we deferring? What do we need more input on?*

Bullet list. Be honest. An amendment with zero open questions is either finished (rare) or hiding something (common).

---

## 9. Alternatives considered

*What other versions of this amendment did we look at, and why did we pick this one?*

For each alternative:
- **What it was**
- **Why we didn't go with it**
- **Under what conditions we'd reconsider**

Link to any competing PR branches (e.g., `amendment-NN/alt-approach`).

## 10. How this would be tested

*How would we know, five years after ratification, whether this amendment is working?*

Define measurable outcomes. Concrete indicators. If we can't say what success looks like, we can't say when to fix it.
